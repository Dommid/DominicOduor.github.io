04 / CYBERSECURITY CASE STUDY CONTROLLED ACADEMIC LABS

Attack simulation.
Defensive insight.

Reproducing adversarial techniques in isolated training environments to understand attack paths, validate security weaknesses, interpret observable evidence, and prioritize protective controls.

Metasploit & MeterpreterWiresharkEttercap / arpspoofBurp SuiteJohn the RipperLinux & Windows
Scope and integrity: This case study summarizes completed academic lab exercises, not an attack on a production organization. Reproduced visuals are conceptual; original credentials, targets, packet captures, and exploit procedures are not published.

01 / PURPOSE

Understand the path.
Strengthen the defense.

The exercises covered three complementary perspectives: vulnerable-host exploitation, network interception and session-security analysis, and a simulated network compromise followed by defensive response.

The goal was to connect observed lab behavior to actionable controls such as vulnerability remediation, secure authentication, encrypted transport, network segmentation, and monitoring.

Source evidence: three completed academic lab reports from February, March, and May 2025. Specific success criteria are reported only where the underlying lab evidence supports them.

02 / METHODOLOGY

From simulation
to mitigation.

High-level process used to evaluate controlled lab scenarios. This is a conceptual workflow, not a reproduction of exploit commands.

01

Scope

Use assigned, isolated lab hosts and stated exercise objectives.

02

Observe

Identify services, host behavior, traffic patterns, and baseline controls.

03

Validate

Exercise documented attack scenarios within the authorized environment.

04

Analyze

Review sessions, captured traffic, response behavior, and evidence limits.

05

Defend

Patch, restrict access, harden transport, and re-test where documented.

03 / DOCUMENTED EXERCISES

Three lab tracks.
One defensive focus.

TRACK AFEB 2025

Vulnerable-host exploitation

Used assigned Windows and Linux training systems to study vulnerability identification, Metasploit/Meterpreter sessions, credential and hash exposure, and classic Heartbleed/Shellshock scenarios.

Evidence documented
  • Meterpreter sessions and account information in the lab
  • Credential/hash analysis with John the Ripper
  • Controlled Heartbleed and Shellshock exercise outputs
Defensive implication: prioritize vulnerable-service patching, credential protection, and limiting administrative exposure.
TRACK BMAR 2025

Packet capture and session security

Examined ICMP and HTTP traffic in Wireshark, simulated ARP poisoning in a LAN lab, and analyzed HTTP/HTTPS requests and session behavior through Burp Suite.

Evidence documented
  • Captured ICMP/HTTP traffic and ARP-poisoning exercises
  • HTTP/HTTPS request and response inspection
  • Proxy certificate and controlled session demonstrations
Defensive implication: protect session tokens, enforce secure transport, and restrict untrusted network interception opportunities.
TRACK CMAY 2025

Simulation and remediation validation

Used an Infection Monkey scenario and a separate Metasploit exercise to examine a vulnerable web server, review remediation recommendations, and evaluate defensive changes.

Evidence documented
  • Simulated exploit and security recommendation records
  • Pre/post Bash version checks and a recorded unsuccessful post-patch exploit attempt
  • Antivirus-test and DMZ firewall-rule exercises
Defensive implication: demonstrate that remediations require verification, not just configuration changes.

05 / TAKEAWAYS

What the exercises
demonstrated.

Security validation: Exposure should be evaluated through observed behavior and confirmed against the exercise scope.

Visibility: Network and application telemetry can help distinguish normal behavior from potentially harmful activity, but context and log quality matter.

Remediation discipline: The May lab documented pre/post patch versions and an unsuccessful exploit attempt after patching in that specific training scenario. This does not establish a broader production security outcome.

Responsible handling: Offensive techniques are described only at a high level; sensitive captured information and exploitable details remain private.
